> Written for ANTHRO-152R: Language, Culture and Education at UCLA, Winter 2026.

I grew up in Almaty, Kazakhstan in a multilingual environment where Russian, Kazakh, and later English all played different roles in my life. At the time, I mostly thought about language in practical terms. Russian was what I spoke at home and in school. Kazakh was the national language that people said was important culturally and politically. English came later and eventually became the language that made international education possible.

Studying anthropology has made it harder for me to think about language purely as a practical tool. The relationship between language, power, and institutions is already well established in anthropology. Because of that, many of the ideas in this course were not completely new to me. What the course did do was provide a clearer framework for thinking about how those dynamics play out specifically within educational systems. Concepts such as language ideology, linguistic capital, and raciolinguistic hierarchies helped me make better sense of experiences that previously just felt normal.

Russian dominated my childhood schooling. Although Kazakhstan officially recognizes both Kazakh and Russian, the linguistic reality in Almaty reflects the country's Soviet past. Most of my teachers and administrators were ethnically Russian, and they spoke a variety of Russian that sounded noticeably different from the Kazakh influenced Russian used in everyday life. Even as a child I subconsciously sensed that difference. Their way of speaking sounded more proper, more educated. Nobody stated this to me explicitly, but I now realize the message was clear through the structure of the institution.

Looking back now, this seems like a straightforward example of language socialization in educational settings. Schools are not just teaching academic content. They are also socializing students into particular linguistic hierarchies (Ochs & Schieffelin, 2009). Certain ways of speaking become associated with authority and competence, while others become marked as informal or less legitimate.

Bourdieu's concept of linguistic capital captures this dynamic quite well. Institutions recognize some language varieties as legitimate and reward them accordingly (Bourdieu, 1986). In my schooling, Russian clearly functioned as that legitimate language. It carried institutional authority, and the specific variety spoken by teachers carried even more symbolic value.

Kazakh occupied a more complicated place in my life. It was explicitly important symbolically. It was tied to national identity and the post-Soviet effort to revive Kazakh culture. But my own relationship with the language was uneven. My first language is Russian, and although I learned some Kazakh as a child, I gradually lost much of it after living abroad.

When visiting relatives in rural areas I sometimes tried to speak Kazakh. Almost always it was met with ridicule. People often laughed at my "Russian" or "urban" pronunciation and vocabulary. What made the situation less welcoming was that code mixing between Russian and Kazakh was actively discouraged. Instead of encouraging partial speakers to communicate however they could to get their point across, the expectation for me was that Kazakh should only be spoken correctly or we shall transition to Russian. That expectation made it easier for me to feel embarrassed than encouraged. Another point of contention for me was that I was actively excluded from conversation in Kazakh, so eventually I completely stopped trying to engage.

In hindsight this seems like a case where language ideologies about linguistic purity can work against language learning itself. Allowing people to mix languages might actually help partial speakers remain engaged. When correctness becomes the priority, participation becomes harder.

The readings helped frame this dynamic through the concept of elite closure. Myers-Scotton argues that language requirements can function as gatekeeping mechanisms that restrict access to certain social and political opportunities (Myers-Scotton, 1993). In contemporary Kazakhstan, Kazakh proficiency is increasingly tied to political legitimacy and certain career paths. At the same time, many people educated during the Soviet period or in urban environments were never given strong institutional support to learn the language. As a result, language becomes both a national symbol and a practical barrier.

My relationship with language shifted significantly when I began studying at an international boarding school in the United Kingdom. Quite obviously, English quickly became the most important language academically. Learning it required a great deal of effort, but it eventually allowed me to participate in international education and later attend university in the United States. In that environment multilingualism was usually framed as an advantage. Students came from many countries, and speaking multiple languages was often interpreted as evidence of being globally oriented or culturally adaptable.

However, one idea that came up repeatedly in this course is that multilingualism is not valued equally across contexts. Bilingualism is often celebrated when it is associated with elite or internationally mobile students but framed as a deficiency when it is associated with marginalized communities. Rosa's work on raciolinguistic ideologies illustrates this dynamic clearly. Bilingualism becomes enrichment when linked to privileged speakers and deficiency when linked to racialized ones (Rosa, 2019).

In my own experience at an international school, bilingualism was usually treated as a sign of cultural sophistication. Yet in other educational contexts, bilingual students may be placed into remedial programs because their language practices are interpreted as evidence of linguistic deficiency rather than competence.

Another dimension that became more visible to me through this course is the relationship between language, race, and expectations about intelligence. As a Kazakh person with East Asian appearance in Western contexts, I have occasionally been racialized as academically gifted. People usually frame this as a compliment. But it also reduces academic success to something supposedly inherent to race rather than something shaped by effort, opportunity, and institutional support.

In a subtle way this kind of stereotype benefits the people making the assumption. If academic outcomes are attributed to race, there is less reason to question how educational systems distribute opportunity in the first place.

The readings on global citizenship education have also enriched the way I think about international schooling. Institutions often frame themselves as producing cosmopolitan or globally minded students. But research on elite education suggests that cosmopolitan identity is frequently tied to class privilege rather than simply mobility or cultural openness (Gilbertson, 2016). Students studying abroad at prestigious institutions are celebrated as global citizens, while migrants moving across borders for work are rarely described in the same way.

Reflecting on my own educational trajectory, it is clear that access to English language education and international schooling provided me with forms of linguistic and cultural capital that are not evenly distributed.

Russian connected me to my childhood environment and family life. English enabled academic mobility and participation in global institutions. Kazakh remains a language that I feel partially disconnected from despite its importance within Kazakhstan's national identity, yet carry shame about not having access to it.

None of these relationships are simply about communication. Language is also about belonging, legitimacy, and access to opportunity. Anthropology has long argued that educational institutions reproduce social hierarchies even while presenting themselves as neutral or merit based (Nieto & Bode, 2013). Thinking about my own educational experiences through this lens makes it much harder to see language education as simply the teaching of grammar or vocabulary. It also highlights how important it is for educators to recognize multilingualism as a resource rather than a deficiency. Students who move between languages or varieties are not lacking linguistic competence, but navigating multiple linguistic systems at once. When schools treat those practices as problems rather than assets, they risk excluding students in ways that mirror the experiences I had with Kazakh. Language education, therefore, is also a process through which institutions determine whose knowledge and whose voice are recognized as legitimate.
